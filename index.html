        Key facts

        About this globe

        COVID-19, week by week

        The pandemic from December 2019 to October 2026 on a 3D globe, as reported to the World Health Organization. Drag to explore while time keeps running, click a country or region for details, compare groups of countries and switch layers in the legend.

        How to read it

        • Red glow: weekly reported cases per million, on a log scale.
        • White columns: weekly reported deaths. The height is proportional to the count.
        • Grey hatching: no weekly report to WHO for over 12 weeks.
        • Grey with fine dots: no cases or deaths in the WHO data, or no separate figures.
        • Teal: share of people with at least one vaccine dose.
        • Glowing blue borders: how strict the government measures were, 2020 to 2022.
        • Key events: the colour shows the type: violet for WHO decisions, pink for new variants, teal for vaccines and treatment, blue for measures, orange for the death toll, amber for other milestones. Recent events stay in a list in the corner for a while; click one to show it again.
        • Flat map: the map button (or G) unrolls the globe into a flat world map and rolls it back up. The map is centred on where you were looking; day and night follow the real sun, with midday in the middle.
        • At the end: “Explore the totals” colours every country by a total for the whole period (deaths or cases per million, vaccination, measures), and “Key facts” sums up the data, with lists you can click through.

        Keyboard

        Space plays or pauses, the arrow keys move one week (with Shift four), / finds a country, I opens this panel, M mutes all sound (the speaker button sets music and sounds separately), F is full screen, G switches between the globe and a flat map and Esc closes a panel. A country panel can copy a link to the country and week.

        What the numbers are, and what they are not

        These are reported figures. They depend on how much each country tested and how it counted and reported, and WHO revises its data over time. Many deaths were never reported: WHO estimates 14.9 million excess deaths in 2020 and 2021 alone (range 13.3 to 16.6 million), about 2.7 times the 5.45 million deaths reported for those two years. From 2023 on, fewer and fewer countries report at all.

        How the data were processed

        • Weekly totals per country, as published by WHO. The picture blends smoothly between weeks, but every number shown is for the last completed week.
        • When a country reports several weeks at once, the glow and columns spread that report over the weeks it covers. One-off jumps in the WHO data (21, probably backlogs of earlier cases or deaths, such as China on 5 February 2023) are drawn at the level of the surrounding weeks. They stay in all totals and are marked in the country panel.
        • Rates per million use Natural Earth population estimates from around 2019.
        • Gaps in the WHO data in 2020 and early 2021 for France, Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan and Montenegro are filled from Johns Hopkins University, as three-week moving averages.
        • Regional figures appear where regional data exist (EU/EEA to mid 2022, about 35 other countries).
        • Vaccination is interpolated between reports and held at the last reported value afterwards. The vaccination data end in August 2024.
        • WHO reports a single figure for China and has no separate entries for Hong Kong SAR, Macao SAR or Taiwan. The globe shows these three areas in grey.

        Country notes and key events

        The notes on how countries fared, and what research points to as reasons, and the key events, were researched and written with AI assistance from the published sources listed with each of them. A second AI pass compared every sentence with its sources; a third checked the sentences that were rewritten after that. Then a person read them all (November 2026). Source pages may have changed since. Milestones of reported deaths and the fading of reporting are computed from the WHO data.
